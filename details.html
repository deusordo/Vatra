How VATRA would work

The proposed model is a small cluster of efficient homes that can run essential utilities locally. Households would make their own decisions and share infrastructure where it costs less or works better together. Modern internet access and links to nearby towns would support work, care, and trade.

Start with land and low demand

Before committing to a site, check the water supply, soil, drainage, legal building and utility options, all-season road access, and internet service. Design homes for the local climate so less energy is needed for heating and cooling. USDA soil survey · U.S. Department of Energy home design guidance

Design for the hardest season

Compare individual power systems with a shared microgrid. Individual systems spread failure risk across homes. A shared system can pool generation and backup, but needs an operator and a way to restore service when shared equipment fails. Size the design for essential loads and the toughest season. Microgrid planning guidance (PDF)

Validate the drinking water source and storage, and compare suitable on-site or shared wastewater options with local specialists. EPA well guidance · EPA wastewater guidance

Households run their own lives

Households manage their homes, work, and daily routines. Start food production with crops that suit the site, a place to store the harvest, and local suppliers for the rest. Keep access to jobs, schools, healthcare, and emergency services.

Neighbors share selected resources

A group of households could share a workshop, tools, or storage. For each shared asset, an agreement would name who can use it, who maintains it, how costs are covered, and what happens if it is unavailable. More complex services would need site-specific design and approval.

For example, households sharing a workshop could agree on booking and training, set aside money for repairs, and arrange backup access to tools. The pilot would record the hours and costs it takes to keep the workshop usable.

Budget for a community people can afford to run

Compare household costs and shared costs before adding residents. Include upkeep, paid maintenance, insurance, and reserves for major replacements. Local businesses could support the budget after they show repeat demand. Household income and trade with nearby towns would remain part of the model.

A pilot proves what can scale

Start with a small group and one shared system. Track the real costs, maintenance time, interruptions, and backup performance through the hardest season. Expand after another household can take over the work from written instructions and recurring income can cover upkeep and replacements.

Land ownership, fees, and decision rules remain open. Those terms would be set out before anyone commits to living at a site.

Featured project

Household systems worksheet

Starter available. Document one routine, such as tracking pantry supplies or checking shared tools. The worksheet creates a one-page record you can keep or print.

  1. List what the routine depends on, who handles it, and where instructions are kept.
  2. Ask another reader to find the instructions and name the next step from the worksheet alone.
  3. Record unclear steps or missing references and update the page.

Lessons from operating communities

Plan around the bad week

Dancing Rabbit describes relying on stored rainwater and facing shortages during drought or heavy use. Record peak demand and a fallback when comparing water options. Dancing Rabbit’s water notes

Make systems easy to inspect

Earthship Biotecture shows visitors the water and power equipment inside a functioning demonstration building. A VATRA demonstration could show how to read the system, who maintains it, and what happens if it fails. Earthship’s visitor center

Separate neighborhood and shared decisions

Earthaven organizes its residential areas into pods and has an association for common land. Dancing Rabbit uses a land trust and describes separate arrangements for vehicles, energy, and village-wide costs. These are examples of how to define who governs and pays for each resource. Earthaven’s ownership and governance · Dancing Rabbit’s co-ops

Evaluate more than the equipment

The Global Ecovillage Network’s design cards ask about culture, economics, community, and ecology. Use the questions to compare options against local conditions and the people who would maintain them. Ecovillage Design Cards

Common questions

Can I visit VATRA or apply to live there?
VATRA is planning a future demonstration site. Public visits and applications depend on a site and an operating plan. The household worksheet is available to try today.
Can I use the worksheet in a rental or an apartment?
Yes. Pick a routine you already manage. For building systems, record where to find the owner’s or manager’s instructions.
What does self-sufficiency mean here?
The goal is to meet more needs close to home while keeping access to local services, trade, and relationships.
Where does the VATRA library fit?
NuMind plans a physical collection for VATRA and offline digital copies of editions that can be shared. See the library plan.
